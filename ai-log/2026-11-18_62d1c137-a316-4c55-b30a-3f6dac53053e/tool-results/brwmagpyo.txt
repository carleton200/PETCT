### ct-beam-energy-spectrum | Claim 1: X-ray beam energy spectrum and effective energy
S: The beam is a mix of photon energies up to the tube voltage (up to 120 keV at 120 kV), and on average it behaves like a single-energy beam of roughly 55 to 70 keV.
V: 55 to 70 [keV (effective energy at 120 kV)] R: Measured effective energy at 120 kV, in air at the scanner center: 54.4 and 62.4 keV on two scanners (one study), 54.9 to 58.0 keV on two other scanners (another study). PET/CT papers use about 70 keV for the beam inside the patient.
SRC: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5966206/ , https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7851788/ , https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&id=9800714&rettype=abstract&retmode=text , https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&id=12931319&rettype=abstract&retmode=text , https://jnm.snmjournals.org/content/45/1_suppl/4S.full.pdf
N: CORRECTED from the draft "about 60 to 70 keV": measured effective energies at 120 kVp are often below 60 keV (54.4 keV and 62.4 keV in PMC5966206; 54.87 to 57.97 keV, mean 56.47, in PMC7851788). The PET/CT literature (Kinahan 1998 abstract: "effective CT photon energy of 70 keV"; Kinahan 2003: "approximately 70 keV"; Townsend 2004: "mean photon energy of 70 keV") uses 70 keV; the average energy rises as the body filters out low-energy photons (beam hardening). "Effective energy" (the single energy with the same half-value layer) and "mean energy" (the average of the spectrum) are different quantities and the sources use them loosely. Kinahan 1998 describes the CT photons as spanning 40 keV through 140 keV. The site tables use 70 keV to match the PET/CT convention; say "about 70 keV" rather than implying it is exact.

### beer-lambert-law | Claim 2: Beer-Lambert law
S: The number of photons that get straight through a material falls off exponentially with thickness: I = I0 x exp(-mu x d), where I0 is the number going in, d is the thickness and mu is the linear attenuation coefficient of the material.
V: I = I0 * exp(-mu * x) [] R: Exact only for a narrow beam of a single energy passing through one uniform material; for a path through several tissues the exponent is the sum (integral) of mu along the path.
SRC: https://www-pub.iaea.org/MTCD/Publications/PDF/Pub1564webNew-74666420.pdf
N: Draft confirmed. IAEA handbook Eq. 11.1 ("Beer's law": I(x) = I0 e^(-mu x)) and Eq. 11.2 for a path through varying tissue. The handbook notes that the law describes only the primary (unscattered) beam and that a real CT beam has many energies, which is the cause of the beam-hardening artifact. The names Beer's law, Beer-Lambert law and exponential attenuation law are all in use. The statement writes the thickness as d only to avoid "x x" in plain text; the usual symbol is x.

### hounsfield-unit-definition | Claim 5: Hounsfield unit definition
S: CT images are stored in Hounsfield units, which compare each tissue with water: HU = 1000 x (mu - mu_water) / mu_water, so 1 HU is a 0.1 percent difference from water.
V: HU = 1000 * (mu - mu_water) / mu_water [HU] R: Some texts write the denominator as (mu_water - mu_air), which fixes air at exactly -1000; because air attenuates about 910 times less than water, the two forms differ by about 1 HU at the air end and not at all at water.
SRC: https://www-pub.iaea.org/MTCD/Publications/PDF/Pub1564webNew-74666420.pdf , https://www.nobelprize.org/uploads/2018/06/hounsfield-lecture.pdf
N: The draft formula, with (mu_water - mu_air) in the denominator, is an accepted variant, but the form with mu_water alone is the one given by the IAEA handbook (Eq. 11.4), which then states HU(air) = -1000 by taking mu_air = 0. With the NIST values at 70 keV the IAEA form gives air = -998.91 HU, so "air is -1000" is a rounded convention under that form and exact under the two-point form; mu_water/mu_air at 70 keV = 914. The scale is named for Godfrey Hounsfield; in his 1979 Nobel lecture he describes the scale running "from air (-1000) at the bottom of the scale, to bone at the top", with water "chosen to be zero".

### why-bone-stops-more-x-rays | Claim 7: why bone stops more X-rays
S: Bone stops more X-rays than soft tissue for two reasons: it is nearly twice as dense, and about a third of its weight is calcium and phosphorus, heavier atoms that absorb X-ray photons outright far more readily than the hydrogen, carbon and oxygen of soft tissue.
V: cortical bone: density 1.92 g/cm3; calcium 22.5% and phosphorus 10.3% by weight [] R: At 70 keV about 30 percent of photon interactions in cortical bone are photoelectric absorption, compared with about 4.7 percent in water; per centimeter, photoelectric absorption is about 17 times more likely in bone than in water.
SRC: https://physics.nist.gov/PhysRefData/XrayMassCoef/tab2.html , https://physics.nist.gov/PhysRefData/XrayMassCoef/ComTab/bone.html , https://physics.nist.gov/cgi-bin/Xcom/xcom2?Method=Mix&Output2=Hand , https://physics.nist.gov/cgi-bin/Xcom/xcom2?Method=Comp&Output2=Hand , https://www-pub.iaea.org/MTCD/Publications/PDF/Pub1564webNew-74666420.pdf

### soft-tissue-mostly-scatters | Claim 7: soft tissue behaves like water and mostly scatters

SRC: https://physics.nist.gov/cgi-bin/Xcom/xcom2?Method=Comp&Output2=Hand , https://physics.nist.gov/cgi-bin/Xcom/xcom2?Method=Mix&Output2=Hand , https://physics.nist.gov/PhysRefData/XrayMassCoef/ComTab/tissue.html , https://physics.nist.gov/PhysRefData/XrayMassCoef/ComTab/muscle.html , https://physics.nist.gov/PhysRefData/XrayMassCoef/ComTab/adipose.html , https://www-pub.iaea.org/MTCD/Publications/PDF/Pub1564webNew-74666420.pdf

### three-fates-of-an-x-ray-photon | Claim 8: what can happen to one X-ray photon in the body
S: Each X-ray photon entering the body does one of three things: it passes straight through and reaches the detector, it is absorbed by an atom and disappears (the photoelectric effect), or it bounces off an electron in a new direction (scattering).
V: three outcomes: transmitted, absorbed, scattered [] R: Two kinds of scattering occur at CT energies: Compton scattering (the photon gives part of its energy to an electron; about 90% of interactions in water at 70 keV) and Rayleigh scattering (the photon changes direction and keeps its energy; about 5.4%). A scattered photon can go on to scatter again, be absorbed, or leave the body.
SRC: https://www-pub.iaea.org/MTCD/Publications/PDF/Pub1564webNew-74666420.pdf , https://physics.nist.gov/cgi-bin/Xcom/xcom2?Method=Comp&Output2=Hand
N: Draft confirmed; Rayleigh (coherent) scattering added as a minor second kind of scatter so the site does not imply Compton is the only one. IAEA handbook chapter 2: photoelectric effect ("The incident photon disappears in the photoelectric interaction"), coherent (Rayleigh) scattering, Compton scattering; a scattered photon may "be followed by a third scatter, photoelectric absorption or no further interactions". Pair production needs at least 1022 keV, so it plays no part in CT or PET. The image is formed from the photons that pass straight through; scattered photons that still reach the detector add haze, which the anti-scatter grid on the detector reduces. "Bounces off an electron" is a simplification that fits Compton scattering; Rayleigh scattering involves the atom as a whole.

### photons-do-not-slow-down | Claim 8: attenuation means fewer photons, not slower ones
S: X-ray photons do not slow down in the body; they all travel at the speed of light, and tissue only reduces how many of them arrive, which is what attenuation means.
V: speed unchanged; number reduced [] R: A Compton-scattered photon does lose energy, but it still travels at the speed of light: a 70 keV photon scattered through 90 degrees leaves with about 61.6 keV, and a 511 keV photon with about 256 keV.
SRC: https://www-pub.iaea.org/MTCD/Publications/PDF/Pub1564webNew-74666420.pdf
N: Draft confirmed. The Beer-Lambert law (IAEA Eq. 11.1) is a statement about the number (intensity) of unscattered photons. Lower photon energy means longer wavelength, not lower speed. The speed statement is standard physics rather than a sentence quoted from the handbook; for X-rays the refractive index of tissue differs from 1 by a few parts per million or less, so "at the speed of light" is accurate for the site. Compton energies computed from E_after = E / (1 + (E / 511 keV) x (1 - cos angle)): 70 keV gives 61.6 keV at 90 degrees and 54.9 keV at 180 degrees; 511 keV gives 256 keV and 170 keV. GAME NOTE: if the site game shows photons slowing down in dense tissue, it should say plainly that this is a visual stand-in for "fewer photons get through", and that real photons never slow.

### photon-pair-loss-in-the-body | Claim 10: size of the attenuation effect at 511 keV
S: A PET event is counted only if both 511 keV photons escape the body, and along a line through 30 cm of tissue only about 1 pair in 18 does, so the correction is large.
V: 5.6 [percent of photon pairs surviving a 30 cm path in water] R: Survival for a total path in water of 10 / 20 / 30 / 40 cm: 38.3 / 14.7 / 5.62 / 2.15 percent (correction factors 2.61 / 6.82 / 17.8 / 46.5). SRC: https://physics.nist.gov/PhysRefData/XrayMassCoef/ComTab/water.html , https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&id=12111133&rettype=abstract&retmode=text , https://jnm.snmjournals.org/content/jnumed/44/11/1855.full.pdf
