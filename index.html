Positron emission tomography + computed tomography

Seeing inside, two ways.

A CT scanner maps the body's structure with X-rays. A PET scanner maps what the body's cells are doing, using a faintly radioactive sugar. A PET/CT scanner does both in one visit and lays the two pictures on top of each other.

The science

Five ideas, each with something to poke at.

One machine, two scanners

From the outside a PET/CT scanner looks like one thick ring. Inside are two machines sharing a bed. At the front, an X-ray tube and a curved detector spin around you several times a second: that is the CT. Behind it sits a fixed ring of crystals that waits for light coming out of your own body: that is the PET.

The bed slides you through the CT first, which takes well under a minute, then parks you in the PET ring for the longer, quieter scan.

A sugar with a tag

Before a PET scan you are injected with a tracer. The usual one is FDG: a glucose molecule with one small group swapped for a radioactive fluorine-18 atom. Cells that burn a lot of sugar, such as many tumours, the brain and the heart, pull it in and cannot get rid of it.

Fluorine-18 does not last. Half of it decays every 110 minutes, so it has to be made in a cyclotron the same day, and by the next morning almost none is left in you.

110 minutes is the half-life of fluorine-18.

Antimatter, briefly

When a fluorine-18 nucleus decays it throws out a positron, the antimatter twin of the electron. The positron wanders less than a millimetre through your tissue before it meets an ordinary electron. The two annihilate: both vanish, and their mass becomes two photons of light.

Those two photons always carry the same energy, 511 keV each, and always leave in almost exactly opposite directions. That back-to-back pair is the signature a PET scanner hunts for.

511 keV is the energy of each photon: the mass of one electron, turned into light.

Catching the pair

The PET ring is thousands of small crystals that flash when a 511 keV photon hits them. When two crystals on opposite sides flash within a few billionths of a second of each other, the scanner assumes they caught one pair, and draws a line between them. The decay happened somewhere on that line.

One line says little. Millions of lines, crossing, say a great deal: they pile up where the tracer has gathered. Fire some decays and watch a hidden hot spot appear.

How CT sees

The CT half works the other way round: it shines X-rays through you and measures what comes out. Each X-ray photon either passes straight through or is stopped, absorbed or knocked off course, and which one happens is a matter of chance.

Dense material with heavy atoms stops far more photons. Bone, full of calcium, casts a strong shadow. Fat, muscle and water cast similar, weaker ones. Air casts almost none. Measure those shadows from hundreds of angles and a computer can work out a cross-section of the body.

Try being one of those photons.

Better together

A PET image alone is a glowing blur: it shows where sugar is being used but not exactly where in the body that is. A CT image alone shows every bone and organ in sharp detail but cannot tell a busy tumour from quiet scar tissue.

Because both scans happen on the same bed minutes apart, the two pictures line up. Laid over each other, the bright spot gets an address. The CT also tells the computer how much tissue each PET photon had to cross, so it can correct for the ones that never made it out.

Reality check

Real photons cannot steer, and they do not slow down inside organs. Each one either gets through or is stopped, by chance, and bone stops far more of them than soft tissue does. That is exactly why bone shows up white on a CT image: it is where the photons went missing. The ones that reach the detector, like you, make the picture.
